# Chapter Drafting Playbook

Use this reference when writing, expanding, revising, or diagnosing a chapter or
scene. The unit can be a short-story section, a web-serial chapter, or a novel
scene sequence.

## Chapter Contract

A chapter is ready to draft when these are known:

- POV and current situation.
- Immediate pressure.
- What changes by the end.
- Reader emotion to deliver.
- One reveal, reversal, payoff, or cost.
- Ending pressure into the next unit.

If any field is missing, infer it from context or ask one focused question when
the choice would affect canon.

## Context Pack

Load only what can prevent errors or improve the current chapter:

- Previous chapter or scene ending.
- Current chapter card or outline.
- Active characters and their current states.
- Relevant relationship tensions.
- Active clues, promises, foreshadowing, and secrets.
- World rules, powers, locations, and timeline constraints.
- User-supplied voice samples or style constraints.

## Opening Moves

Make the first page create motion. Choose one move that fits the genre:

- Begin during action, pursuit, confrontation, discovery, or aftermath.
- Show an abnormal detail inside an otherwise normal setting.
- Start with a charged line of dialogue.
- Put a deadline on the scene.
- Reveal an object, message, body, debt, injury, or missing person.
- Force an immediate choice.
- Show betrayal, reversal, or public humiliation.
- Flash forward to a consequence, then return to how it happened.

Avoid slow weather, routine waking, travel logistics, lore lectures, and summary
of the previous chapter unless they are immediately turned into conflict.

## Chapter Shape

A reliable commercial chapter shape:

1. Opening disturbance: the reader knows why this moment matters.
2. First tactic: the POV character tries the obvious move.
3. Complication: new resistance, information, or social cost.
4. Tactic shift: the character adapts, escalates, lies, risks, or sacrifices.
5. Payoff or reversal: one promise is answered or pressure changes form.
6. Hook: a new question, danger, choice, or emotional break opens forward motion.

Not every chapter needs a battle or revelation, but every chapter needs a
noticeable change.

## Ending Hook Families

Use an earned hook. Do not withhold information artificially.

- Fact hook: a new fact changes interpretation.
- Danger hook: threat arrives or safety collapses.
- Interrupted-action hook: a crucial action begins but its result is delayed.
- Identity/status hook: someone's role, rank, allegiance, or public image changes.
- Dilemma hook: every option costs something.
- Clue hook: an object, phrase, record, or detail points elsewhere.
- Deadline hook: time pressure becomes explicit.
- Threat/promise hook: a character commits to future action.
- Absence hook: someone or something should be present but is gone.
- Subtext hook: dialogue means more than it says.
- Image hook: a recurring object or setting returns with new meaning.
- Emotional hook: a relationship line breaks, turns, or becomes unavoidable.

## Expanding Without Padding

When a chapter is too thin, diagnose the missing layer:

- Too little plot: add a causal beat, obstacle, tactical shift, or consequence.
- Too little emotion: slow the key choice, add embodied reaction, or put the
  emotion into dialogue and behavior.
- Too little texture: add specific objects, sounds, light, smell, spatial
  pressure, weather only if it affects action, and material constraints.
- Too little relationship: add a status contest, debt, secret asymmetry,
  misunderstanding, intimacy, or changed leverage.
- Too little suspense: add a promise, clue, deadline, or gap between what the
  reader knows and what a character knows.

Good expansion changes the scene. Bad expansion explains the scene.

## Dialogue

Dialogue should do at least one job:

- Change power.
- Hide or reveal information.
- Test loyalty, desire, fear, or status.
- Force a decision.
- Create intimacy, distance, humiliation, or danger.
- Contradict what the character does.

Natural dialogue is partial. People dodge, interrupt, understate, overcompensate,
change topics, and let silence carry pressure. Use action beats when they alter
meaning, not as filler after every line.

## Chinese Prose Naturalization

When drafting or polishing Chinese fiction:

- Keep paragraph rhythm readable on mobile: shorter paragraphs for action and
  tension, longer ones only when the thought or image earns it.
- Prefer concrete verbs and visible behavior over explanatory emotion labels.
- Let idiom and register match character background; do not make every narrator
  sound formally polished.
- Remove translated cadence: avoid overuse of abstract connectors, symmetrical
  sentence pairs, and "he realized/felt/understood" summaries.
- Use punctuation to carry rhythm; avoid stacking quotation, ellipsis, and
  exclamation as a substitute for pressure.
- Avoid dash-heavy cadence. In Chinese prose, `—` and `——` should almost never
  be the default way to signal reversal, hesitation, or drama.

## Dash Discipline

Before finalizing drafted prose:

1. Search mentally or with tooling for `—`, `——`, and `--`.
2. Delete every dash that only creates a dramatic pause.
3. Keep a dash only for an actual interruption, broken speech, or abrupt turn.
4. Prefer sentence breaks, paragraph breaks, action beats, silence, direct
   dialogue, or visible consequence.
5. For Chinese prose, keep dash use below one construction per 1,000 Chinese
   characters unless the user has explicitly requested that punctuation style.

## De-Generic Prose Pass

Run this pass when text sounds AI-generated, over-smooth, or interchangeable:

1. Replace broad labels with scene-specific behavior.
2. Cut repeated emotional explanation after the reader can infer it.
3. Vary sentence length according to pressure.
4. Add one concrete object or physical action that can only belong to this scene.
5. Give each major speaker a different strategy, vocabulary, or avoidance habit.
6. Keep imperfection: unfinished thoughts, wrong assumptions, awkward pauses,
   selfish motives, and small contradictions.

## Chapter Gate

Before calling a chapter done, check:

- Opening disturbance appears early.
- POV, tense, and naming match established canon.
- At least one meaningful state changed.
- Reader-promise emotion is present on the page.
- Dialogue changes pressure rather than only delivering information.
- Description affects mood, action, clueing, or character.
- Ending hook follows from the chapter rather than feeling pasted on.
- Continuity changes are recorded if working in a project.
- If a word target exists, the count is checked with tooling where practical.
