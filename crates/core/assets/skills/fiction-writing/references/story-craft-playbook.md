# Story Craft Playbook

Use this reference when the task needs deeper story architecture, genre diagnosis,
character arc design, suspense engineering, or revision planning. Treat these as
lenses, not rules to impose on working material.

## Story Stack

Build from the smallest truthful promise upward:

1. Reader promise: the recurring experience the reader returns for.
2. Emotional engine: the pressure that repeatedly produces that experience.
3. Premise: protagonist + desire + opposition + stakes + irony.
4. Dramatic question: the question the ending must answer.
5. Character engine: want, need, wound, mask, leverage, and costly choice.
6. Plot engine: escalating attempts, reversals, reveals, consequences, and hooks.
7. Continuity engine: facts, timeline, active promises, states, and constraints.

For commercial or serialized fiction, keep visible progress in nearly every unit:
status, power, clue, intimacy, danger, resource, reputation, or commitment.

## Outline Depths

- Premise: one sentence with protagonist, desire, obstacle, stakes, and irony.
- Logline: premise plus hook, genre promise, and central dramatic question.
- Synopsis: beginning, middle, end, and transformation in 3-8 paragraphs.
- Arc map: acts/volumes, pressure shifts, reversals, and payoff schedule.
- Beat sheet: 12-40 causal turns, each changing pressure or information.
- Chapter card: chapter purpose, POV, setting, conflict, reveal, emotional turn,
  continuity change, and hook.
- Scene card: POV, time/place, want, obstacle, tactic, turn, cost, next pressure.
- Continuity bible: canon facts, timeline, character state, relationships,
  unresolved promises, clues, rules, and revealed information.

## Structure Lenses

Use a structure lens only when it clarifies a current problem.

### Three-Act

Best for mainstream novels, film-like pacing, commercial arcs.

- Act 1: setup, inciting incident, refusal/complication, first commitment.
- Act 2A: trials, new tactics, midpoint revelation or false victory.
- Act 2B: pressure rises, losses compound, protagonist's old strategy fails.
- Act 3: final plan, climax choice, cost, resolution.

### Seven-Point

Best for diagnosing causal movement.

- Hook: status quo and character deficit.
- Plot turn 1: external pressure forces action.
- Pinch 1: antagonist/system shows real threat.
- Midpoint: protagonist shifts from reactive to active.
- Pinch 2: cost becomes personal.
- Plot turn 2: final clue, loss, or self-knowledge unlocks the ending.
- Resolution: answer the dramatic question.

### Commercial Beat Sheet

Best for pacing checks and reader expectation management.

- Opening image, theme pressure, setup, catalyst, debate, commitment.
- Promise-of-the-premise sequence, relational/subplot pressure, midpoint turn.
- Compounding opposition, apparent defeat, reframe, final plan, finale, final image.

### Quest / Mythic Arc

Best for fantasy, adventure, training, pilgrimage, and identity arcs.

- Ordinary pattern, call, refusal/constraint, mentor/rule discovery, threshold.
- Tests, allies, enemies, ordeal, reward, road back, final transformation.

### Contrast Turn

Best for slice-of-life, literary, puzzle, and low-combat stories.

- Establish a pattern.
- Develop it with variation.
- Introduce a turn that recontextualizes the pattern.
- Resolve by synthesis, acceptance, rupture, or ironic return.

### Serial Progression

Best for web novels, long serials, and chapter-by-chapter production.

- Immediate hook and readable goal.
- Frequent micro-payoffs.
- Recurring cast pressure.
- Visible progression track: skill, clue, relationship, status, territory, money,
  rank, reputation, or danger.
- Endings that close one beat while opening the next pressure.

## Genre Architecture

- Mystery: central question, suspect web, clue ladder, red herrings, midpoint
  reframe, solution that makes prior clues feel inevitable.
- Thriller: visible clock, escalating threat, pursuit/reversal, narrowing options,
  moral cost, final confrontation.
- Romance: collision, attraction with incompatibility, false intimacy, barrier
  exposed, rupture, earned choice.
- Fantasy: wonder promise, rule discovery, faction pressure, power cost, worldview
  expansion, climactic use or violation of a learned rule.
- Science fiction: speculative premise, implication chain, technical/social
  constraint, unintended consequence, idea-driven climax.
- Horror: normal rupture, rule discovery, dread escalation, isolation,
  transgression/revelation, survival with cost.
- Coming-of-age: lack/need, boundary crossing, disillusionment,
  self-definition, irreversible maturity cost.
- Historical: period pressure, social rules, material constraints, power structure,
  private desire versus public cost.
- Xianxia/wuxia/progression fantasy: weak-to-strong ladder, rule-bound power
  system, sect/faction pressure, resources, reputation, breakthrough cost.
- Urban reversal/face-slap: underestimation, public pressure, evidence gap,
  timed reveal, cathartic status shift, next stronger opponent.
- Rebirth/regression: future knowledge constraint, altered choice, butterfly
  consequence, debt/justice list, emotional repair or revenge ledger.

## Character Arc Checks

- Want: what the character actively pursues.
- Need: what must change internally.
- Wound/belief: the false rule shaping choices.
- Mask: behavior used to survive.
- Leverage: what another character or system can use against them.
- Pressure test: a scene that makes the old strategy fail.
- Choice: a costly decision proving change or tragic refusal.

Design characters through contradiction, not labels. A useful character profile
contains the behavior readers see, the motive underneath it, the contradiction
that creates tension, and the scene type that exposes it.

## Relationship Design

Track relationships as moving states:

- Attraction/repulsion: what pulls them together and what makes proximity costly.
- Power balance: who has leverage, information, status, or emotional control.
- Secret asymmetry: what one person knows, hides, misunderstands, or refuses.
- Public/private gap: how the relationship looks versus what it means.
- Turning points: scenes where trust, debt, desire, fear, or obligation changes.

Every recurring relationship should have at least one repeatable pressure source,
not just a label like friend, rival, lover, mentor, or family.

## Suspense And Payoff System

Separate promises by lifespan:

- Micro promise: resolved within a scene or chapter.
- Short promise: resolved in 2-3 chapters.
- Medium promise: resolved across a sequence or act.
- Long promise: resolved near the climax or series endpoint.

Strong hooks usually do one of five jobs:

- Reveal a fact that changes what the reader thought was true.
- Create urgent danger or a deadline.
- Interrupt an action before its consequence lands.
- Force a choice with no clean option.
- Show a gap between what a character says, knows, and does.

Payoffs need preparation. Before delivering a twist, check that the story planted
evidence, misdirected attention fairly, and made the reveal change decisions.

## Scene Diagnostics

Use this checklist when a scene feels flat:

1. Can the POV character's immediate want be stated in one sentence?
2. Is there an obstacle with agency, force, or cost?
3. Does the tactic change at least once?
4. Is there a turn the reader can feel?
5. Does the ending create a consequence, question, or new pressure?
6. Could the scene be removed without changing plot, relationship, emotion, or
   theme? If yes, merge or cut it.

## Revision Passes

- Macro pass: premise, dramatic question, causal chain, stakes, ending.
- Reader-promise pass: whether the story delivers the expected recurring emotion.
- Arc pass: protagonist want/need, antagonist/system pressure, relationship movement.
- Sequence pass: turning points, reversals, payoffs, unresolved promises.
- Scene pass: goal, conflict, turn, consequence, hook, pacing.
- Continuity pass: timeline, names, rules, wounds, promises, revealed information.
- Prose pass: POV filter, concrete nouns/verbs, rhythm, dialogue subtext,
  paragraph shape, and generic phrase removal.

## Diagnosis Shortcuts

- "Nothing happens": identify the missing change, then add a decision or
  consequence rather than more description.
- "Too much happens": group beats by cause and cut events that do not change
  pressure or state.
- "Characters feel thin": replace adjectives with choices under pressure,
  habits, contradictions, and relationships with leverage.
- "Chapter feels slow": move the disturbance earlier, reduce setup, and add
  a mid-chapter tactic shift.
- "Ending is weak": close a small promise and open a sharper one.
- "Prose feels generic": add scene-specific objects, imperfect dialogue,
  asymmetric reactions, and concrete behavior.
